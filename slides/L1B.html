Your First Task at Work

build on top of an LLM

  Your company has 3,000 internal PDFs
      policies · specifications · meeting notes

  Build something that answers staff questions about them.

Section A told you what an LLM is. Section B tells you how to use one.

Section A was the component: what a language model is, and how one is built. Section B is the job — and the job is almost never building a model.

The task on the slide is a real one, and a common first assignment. Three thousand internal documents that nobody can find anything in, and a request to make them answerable. Nothing about it is exotic; every organisation has this pile.

One example carries the whole of this lecture, and each topic arrives because the previous attempt broke. You ask the model directly and it invents an answer, so you write a better instruction. The instruction makes it honest but no better informed, so you give it the documents. The documents come back cut in the wrong places, so you look at how you cut them. That ordering is not a narrative device — it is genuinely the order in which you meet these problems.

Just Ask It

  You:    According to our leave policy, how many days of annual leave
          does a new joiner get?

  Model:  New joiners are typically entitled to 14 days of annual leave in
          their first year, accruing at 1.17 days per month, with pro-rata
          adjustment for mid-year starters.

  Your actual policy: 12 days, no accrual, available from day one.

Ask the model directly and you get a fluent, confident, completely invented answer.

It has never seen your company's documents.

The obvious first attempt is to ask the model the question.

What comes back is fluent, confident, and completely invented. The dangerous part is the detail: "14 days", "accruing at 1.17 days per month", "pro-rata adjustment for mid-year starters". That is not a vague answer, it is a specific one, and it is wrong in every particular. The real policy is 12 days, no accrual, available from day one.

This is hallucination, and it is not a lapse of care or a wording bug. Section A's claim was that a language model produces the most plausible continuation of the text so far. A plausible-sounding leave policy is exactly what that produces. Plausible is not the same as true, and the model has no way to tell the two apart here, because it has never seen your company's documents.

Try to find a number in that answer the model could possibly have known. There isn't one.

Modify Your Prompt

the same question as 2:1, asked better

  You:    According to our leave policy, how many days of annual
          leave does a new joiner get? If you do not know, say so.

  Model:  I haven't been given your leave policy, so I can't tell you. 
          Share the document and I'll answer from it.

                                          Honest — but still no answer.

Change what you say before you change the model. Prompting Is the cheapest thing that works.
Give examples (few-shot), ask for the steps (chain of thought), fix the output shape, say what to do when it does not know.

Before changing anything about the model, change what you say to it.

One added instruction — answer only from documents you have been given, and say so if you have not been given them — turned a confident invention into an honest refusal. It cost nothing, took a second, and can be undone as easily. That is the case for trying the prompt first, always: it is the free experiment you run before you spend anything.

The other techniques on the slide are the same kind of move. Few-shot: show two or three worked examples of the answer you want, rather than describing it. Chain of thought: ask for the steps rather than the conclusion. Fixing the output shape: say you want JSON with these fields, or three bullets, not prose. And saying what to do when it does not know: give it permission to refuse.

Then the limit, which this example makes easy to see. Prompting changes behaviour, never knowledge. The model is now honest about not knowing your leave policy. It still does not know it, and no wording will put it there. Getting the right answer is the next section.

Be straight about how far a good instruction goes, too: it makes refusing more likely, not certain. The model can still invent inside any shape you give it — a JSON field with a declared type is just as easy to fill with a wrong number as a sentence is. Prompting reduces the problem; only giving the model the document removes the cause.

Give It the Documents

the same question as 2:1, with the chunk attached

  Retrieved from  hr-leave-policy-2026.pdf, p.4:

      "Annual leave entitlement begins on the first day of employment.
       Grade 1–3: 12 days. No accrual period applies."

  You:    According to our leave policy, how many days of annual leave
          does a new joiner get?

  Model:  12 days, available from your first day — there is no accrual
          period.  [hr-leave-policy-2026.pdf, p.4]

Cut the documents into chunks. Find the few that might contain the answer, paste them into the prompt, and ask the question with them attached.

RAG — retrieval-augmented generation.

The fix is not a better model or a cleverer instruction. It is putting the answer in front of it.

Cut the documents into chunks — short passages, a paragraph or two each. When a question arrives, find the few chunks that might contain the answer, paste them into the prompt above the question, and ask. The model reads them the way it reads anything else in its prompt and answers from what is there. It can cite the file and the page, because the file and the page came in attached to the chunk.

That is RAG — retrieval-augmented generation. Same question as before, same model, one extra paragraph in the prompt, and the answer is now correct and checkable. Nothing was retrained.

It exists for one reason: the model does not know your organisation's documents and never will, however large it gets. They were not in its training data, they are not public, and they change every month. Everything else about RAG is engineering.

"Find the chunks that might contain the answer" is doing a lot of work in that sentence, and it does it with embeddings from Section A. Text becomes vectors, similar meanings land near each other, so the question's vector finds the passages that are about the same thing — which is why embeddings were worth an entire section.

The RAG Pipeline

flowchart TB
    subgraph OFFLINE ["once, in advance"]
        direction TB
        P["3,000 PDFs"] --> CH["chunk<br/>cut into short pieces"]
        CH --> EMB["embed<br/>each chunk → a vector"]
        EMB --> VS[("vector store")]
    end
    subgraph ONLINE ["every question"]
        direction TB
        Q["question"] --> QE["embed the question"]
        QE --> RET["retrieve nearest chunks"]
        RET --> ASM["paste into the prompt"]
        ASM --> GEN["model generates the answer"]
        GEN --> ANS["answer + sources"]
    end
    VS -.-> RET

Once, in advance: chunk → embed → store.

Every question: embed → retrieve → assemble → generate.

The pipeline has two halves, and running them together in your head is the most common confusion.

The top half happens once, in advance, and then on a schedule whenever the documents change. Take the three thousand PDFs, cut each one into chunks, run every chunk through an embedding model, and store the resulting vectors. For three thousand documents that is a job you leave running overnight. Nobody waits for it.

The bottom half happens on every question, in the couple of hundred milliseconds while a user watches a cursor blink. Embed the question with the same embedding model, find the nearest chunks in the store, paste them into the prompt, generate, return the answer with its sources. Only the question and the answer are new work; the expensive part was done last night.

Two things in this diagram are decisions rather than settings, and neither has a default answer. How big is a chunk: too small and the answer gets separated from the context that gives it meaning, too large and most of what you retrieve is padding you pay for. How many chunks do you retrieve: too few and you miss the answer, too many and you bury it. Both are trade-offs you settle by measuring, which is what the evaluation section later is for.

RAG Can Still Fail

the answer was in your PDF; RAG lost it on the way

  Your policy PDF

      Annual leave entitlement
      ─────────────────────────
      Grade 1–3 ........... 12 days
      Grade 4–6 ........... 18 days <-- chunk cuts here
      Grade 7+  ........... 25 days <-- a new chunk starts


  Chunking cut the table off from its heading, so the chunk that reached
  the model was only:

      "Grade 7+ ........... 25 days"


  Q    "How much annual leave does a Grade 7 employee get?"

  A    "I'm sorry — the documents don't say."

The retrieval step is only as good as the way you cut up the documents.

Chunking, retrieval and generation each fail in their own way.

RAG is not a solved problem, and this is the failure that surprises people most: the answer was in your PDF, and the assistant still could not give it.

The policy table lists entitlement by grade. Chunking cut it between the Grade 4–6 row and the Grade 7+ row, so the chunk that reached the model was a single orphan line — 25 days — with no heading above it to say what those 25 days are. Retrieval did its job. The model behaved correctly on what it was given. The information was destroyed on the way in, before either of them saw it.

Tables and forms split badly, and internal documents are mostly tables and forms: fee schedules, org charts, spec sheets, appendices. This is not an exotic case, it is the normal one.

Chunking, retrieval and generation each fail in their own way, and the difficulty is that from the outside all three look identical — a wrong or missing answer. Telling them apart is a real skill, and it is the difference between fixing a chunking bug in an afternoon and spending a month swapping models for a problem the model never had.

The instinct that usually surfaces first — repeat the heading into every chunk — is the right one, and there is a whole family of variations on it.

Train a Model Using Our Documents?

continued pretraining: raw text, same next-token objective, no pairs

  What the model already read
      ~10,000,000,000,000 tokens     the public web, books, code

  What you have
      ~25,000,000 tokens             your 3,000 PDFs

  Your entire archive is one part in four hundred thousand.

  One pass over it barely moves a weight.
  Enough passes to memorise it damages everything else.

Continued pretraining works — at a scale you do not have.

And a fact inside the model has no source, goes out of date, and is readable by everyone.

RAG can lose an answer, and the instinct that follows is a strong one: stop working around the model and change the model. Take the three thousand PDFs and train on them.

Hold that instinct against what actually broke. The grade 7 answer was lost because a table got cut away from its heading. That is a bug in your pipeline, with a pipeline fix — keep the heading with the rows — and no amount of training repairs it. Nothing in this section is the answer to the previous section's example.

Follow the instinct anyway, because there genuinely is a second strategy here and you need to know what it is for. It just is not the answer to this.

That is a real technique with a real name — continued pretraining, sometimes domain-adaptive pretraining. Raw text, the same next-token objective the model was originally trained with, no question-and-answer pairs involved. It is how the domain-specialised models were built, so this is not a naive idea.

It is a scale idea, though, and the numbers are brutal. A modern base model has read something on the order of ten trillion tokens. Three thousand PDFs is around twenty-five million — one part in four hundred thousand of what it has already seen. Train one pass over that and essentially nothing changes. Train enough passes to force it to memorise, and you damage everything the model knew before — the standard name for that is catastrophic forgetting. To make the facts stick you would also have to restate each one many different ways, because a detail seen once in one phrasing is recalled unreliably — so the amount of text you actually need is far larger than the amount you have.

And suppose it worked. You still have three problems that retrieval does not have. No source: what comes out is a fluent sentence with no file and no page behind it. It goes stale: the policy is amended and the model is confidently wrong until the next training run, where retrieval just needs the file replaced. No permissions: once a document is in the weights, there is no way to keep it from a reader who should not see it, whereas retrieval filters at the moment of the question.

So continued pretraining is for organisations with billions of tokens of their own text and a reason to own a model. It is not the answer to "our RAG missed one." But it does raise the right question — if raw documents are the wrong input, what is the right input for training?

Supervised Fine Tuning (SFT)

one training example is one pair, and you write it

  prompt      "How many days of annual leave does a grade 5 joiner get?"
  response    "18 days, available from your first day.
               [hr-leave-policy-2026.pdf, p.4]"

  prompt      "Can I carry leave into next year?"
  response    "Yes — up to 5 days, and they must be used by 31 March.
               [hr-leave-policy-2026.pdf, p.7]"

  ... a few hundred to a few thousand more

  From 1,000 pairs it reliably learns the format, the citation habit,
  the tone, and how to refuse. It may also memorise those 1,000 answers,
  which is not the same as knowing your policy:

      "grade 5 joiner?"      18 days    you wrote this pair
      "grade 6?"             ?          you did not. It answers anyway.
      policy changes to 20   18 days    confidently, until you retrain

SFT — supervised fine-tuning. The data is prompt-and-response pairs, written by you.

500 to 5,000 pairs is enough to change the manner. The material is a different problem.

This is the right input for training, and it is not documents. Almost every time someone says "fine-tuning" they mean SFT — supervised fine-tuning — and its training data is pairs: a prompt, and the response you wanted. You write them, or you generate them and then check them.

The quantity surprises people. Not millions — a few hundred to a few thousand. A well-known result from 2023 took a large base model and a thousand carefully written pairs and got a strong instruction-following assistant out of it. A production fine-tune for a house voice or a fixed output format is routinely built on a few hundred. Teaching a genuinely new task runs to tens of thousands. Only building a general-purpose assistant from nothing needs millions, and that is not a job you will be given.

Now put those two numbers side by side, because together they explain everything about what this strategy is good for. Twenty-five million tokens of your documents barely dents what the model knows. A thousand pairs reliably changes how it answers. Same weights, same machinery, same training procedure — the difference is entirely what you are trying to teach.

The reason is in the data. Format, tone, vocabulary and the habit of citing a source appear in every single pair, a thousand times over, so they are absorbed almost for free. A given fact appears in exactly one pair, so it is learned weakly at best. Manner is the cheapest thing to teach this way; matter is the most expensive.

Be precise about "it does not learn the facts", though, because the honest version is more interesting. Ask that trained model about a grade 5 joiner and it will very likely say 18 days — you wrote that pair, and a thousand pairs seen several times over is enough to memorise a short answer. So it can repeat what you wrote down. That is not the same as knowing the policy, and the difference shows up the moment you step outside your own pairs.

Ask it about a grade 6 joiner. Also 18 days — obvious to any human looking at the policy table, and absent from your pairs. The model answers anyway, with a plausible number, because that is what it always does. Ask about grade 2. Same problem. Then the policy is amended to 20 days: your model keeps saying 18, in your house format, with a citation, until somebody runs training again — and there is no way to check which of the thousand baked-in facts have gone out of date.

Worst of all, memorised and invented answers come out looking identical. Same tone, same confidence, same citation, because you trained all three of those. The fine-tune made the wrong answers more convincing, not less.

And the arithmetic finishes it. Covering the policy properly means one pair per fact, so covering three thousand documents means writing out every fact in them as a pair — at which point you have hand-built an index that is worse than the real one: it cannot cite, it cannot be updated, and it is stale the day you finish.

One warning on the way out, visible in the example pairs. Every response there cites a file and a page. After a thousand of those, the model cites a file and a page — whether or not it has one. Fine-tuning can teach the appearance of grounding to a model that is not grounded. Only retrieval puts a real source behind it.

Recap: Three Strategies

diagnose first; then the cheapest strategy that fits

What is wrong The strategy What it takes
It ignores your instructions, or answers in the wrong shape change what you say — the prompt free, instant, easy to undo
It does not have the information change what it can see — RAG a pipeline to build; no training
Its manner is wrong and instructions will not hold it change how it behaves — SFT on pairs pairs to write, a training run, an eval set

Three strategies, not three stages. They fix different problems, so one does not rescue another — and the prompt, being free, is always worth trying first.

RAG changes what it knows. Fine-tuning changes how it behaves.

Not strategies: swapping the model — too cheap to count. Pretraining your own — it needs far more text than you have.

Three strategies, and this table is the skeleton of the rest of the course.

They are three separate strategies, not three stages of one. Read the table top to bottom and it looks like an escalation — try this, and if it fails try that — and that reading will cost you months. If RAG cannot answer a question, fine-tuning will not answer it either; the information still is not there. If the model's format is wrong, retrieving more documents does nothing at all. These three address different faults, and the expensive one is not a stronger version of the cheap one.

So the skill is diagnosis before selection. Read the failure and say what kind it is. Did it ignore what you asked for, or answer in the wrong shape? That is a prompt problem. Did it not have the information? That is a retrieval problem, and no wording and no training will conjure the fact. Is its manner wrong in a way instructions keep failing to hold — the format drifts, the tone is off, it will not stay consistent across a thousand answers? That is the one fine-tuning is for.

That is not an abstract skill, and this lecture has already run one case of it. What actually fixed the grade 7 answer? Repeating the heading into every chunk — a change to how the documents were cut up, made in an afternoon, in the pipeline where the bug was. Not a better prompt, not a fine-tune, not a bigger model. A retrieval fault, diagnosed as one, fixed where it lived. The expensive version of that afternoon is the team that reads the same failure, decides the model is not smart enough, and spends a month training one.

There is a one-line version of the table worth memorising, because it is the most common LLM-engineering interview question there is: RAG changes what the model knows, fine-tuning changes how it behaves. "Knows" is shorthand — strictly, RAG changes what the model can see at the moment you ask — but that is the sentence people say. Most candidates answer this badly, and they all fail the same way: fine-tuning looks like how you teach a model your documents. It is the expensive wrong answer. Retrieval is the cheap right one.

There is a cost order inside the table, and it is worth using, but only as a tie-breaker. Where two strategies could both address your fault, take the cheaper one — which is why the prompt is always the first thing you try. It is free, it is reversible, and it partly helps everything. It is not the first step of a sequence; it is the free experiment you run before you spend money.

Two things are deliberately not on this table, for opposite reasons.

Choosing a different model is left off because it is too cheap to be a decision. It used to be a serious one and now it is a line in a config file — swap it, run your evaluation, keep whichever wins. The thing to remember is that every number you measured belongs to the model you measured it on, so after a swap you measure again.

Continued pretraining is left off because it is not a strategy available to you. It needs a thousand times more text than you have, and it belongs to a different kind of organisation. If a fine-tune did not solve your problem, the answer is essentially never "now pretrain your own model" — it is that you diagnosed the fault wrongly.

At this point the assistant answers questions correctly. Which is exactly when people start asking it to do things.

An Agent Is Three Things

what "a tool" actually is, and one turn of the loop

flowchart LR
    T["think:<br/>what do I need?"] --> C["call a tool"]
    C --> R["read the result"]
    R -- "not done yet" --> T
    R -- "done" --> A["answer"]

  Its tools — some come with it, some you add:
      search_policy(query)          → returns policy text
      get_leave_balance(staff_id)   → returns a number
      submit_leave(staff_id, date)  → files the request

  User:   "File a leave request for me for next Monday."

  think   I need to know if they have leave left.
  call    get_leave_balance("E4471")
  read    → 7.5 days
  think   Enough. Book it.
  call    submit_leave("E4471", "2026-09-07")
  read    → confirmation #LR-8823
  answer  "Booked for Monday 7 September. You have 6.5 days left."

model + tools + a loop

Each tool is described to the model in words. It decides when to call one.

An agent is three things: a model, some tools, and a loop.

A tool is just a function described to the model in words — its name, what it does, what arguments it takes, what it gives back. Three of them here: one that searches policy text, one that looks up a leave balance, one that files a request. The model never sees the code, only the description.

Some tools come with the assistant you are using — Claude Code arrives knowing how to read a file and run a command, and nobody handed it those. Others you write yourself, which is what you are doing here. The definition does not care which: a model that can use tools, read what comes back and keep going is an agent either way.

The loop is the whole mechanism. The model thinks about what it needs, calls one tool, reads the result, and decides whether it is finished. If not, round again. Follow the trace: it does not know the leave balance, so it asks for it; seven and a half days is enough; so it files the request and reports the confirmation number. Each step is chosen after the previous result comes back, which is what makes this a loop and not a script — nobody wrote "check the balance, then submit" anywhere.

Notice what changed on this slide compared with every slide before it. Until now the system only ever produced text. Now it writes to something: a leave request exists in the HR system that did not exist a moment ago, and no sentence can un-file it. That is the entire reason the next slide exists.

Loops Fail in Specific Ways

Failure Why it happens
Picks the wrong tool it only has your description to go on
Never stops keeps retrying a failing call
Runs out of context every step is appended to the prompt
Invents an argument passes a staff ID that does not exist

Agent demos are easy. Agent reliability is hard.

Each failure has a standard fix.

Agent demos are easy and agent reliability is hard, and the gap between the two is most of the work and most of the job.

Each row is a failure that appears as soon as a demo meets real users. It picks the wrong tool, because a one-line description is all it has to go on and two of your tools sound alike. It never stops, because a call keeps failing and retrying looks like progress from the inside. It runs out of context, because every thought, call and result is appended to the prompt, so a long loop eventually exceeds what the model can hold. And it invents an argument — a staff ID that does not exist — for the same reason it invented a leave policy earlier: a plausible-looking ID is easy to produce.

Every one of these has a standard fix, and those fixes are the content of the agents topic later in the course.

The running example is what makes this urgent rather than academic. A wrong sentence is embarrassing. A wrong submit_leave call is an HR incident.

Reasoning Models

flowchart LR
    Q["question"] --> S["standard model<br/>answers immediately"]
    Q --> R["reasoning model<br/>works through it first"]
    S --> S2["fast · cheap<br/>fine for most things"]
    R --> R2["slow · expensive<br/>better on hard problems"]

  WORTH IT
    "Given our leave policy, our public holiday calendar, and this
     employee's start date, how much leave can they take before December?"
    — several steps, each depending on the last

  NOT WORTH IT
    "What is our office address?"
    — you paid for thinking, and got the same answer, slower

Let the model spend more compute before it answers.

The judgement call is when that is worth it — and often it is not.

A reasoning model is one trained to spend compute working a problem through before it answers, rather than answering immediately. The thinking is itself generated text — the model works on paper first, and the paper costs tokens.

That is a trade, not an upgrade. It is slower and it costs more, sometimes by an order of magnitude, because you pay for every token of thinking. And the accuracy gain only appears on problems that genuinely have several steps, each depending on the last.

The two examples are the judgement call. Working out how much leave someone can take before December means combining the policy, the holiday calendar and a start date, in that order: worth it. Looking up the office address is one lookup and a sentence — you paid for thinking and got the same answer, slower.

So the engineering question is not "is the reasoning model better". It is "which of my requests deserve it", and in most systems the answer is a small minority of them, routed there deliberately. That is a decision with a number attached, which is what the next section is about.

It Works. Does It?

  You tried    9 questions.

  Your users will ask    9,000.

"It looks fine" is not evaluation.

You have tried it, and it works. On the questions you thought of — nine of them, maybe a dozen.

Your users will ask thousands, in phrasings you did not anticipate, about documents you have never read. Whatever number of test questions feels like enough right now is almost certainly too small.

"It looks fine" is a demo, not evidence. It tells you the system can work. It tells you nothing about how often it does.

What Evaluation Actually Is

built once, by hand, from real staff questions

  Your test set — 150 questions you wrote, with known answers:

    Q: "How many days leave for a grade 5 joiner?"     A: 18
    Q: "Can I carry leave into next year?"             A: yes, max 5 days
    Q: "Who approves leave over 10 days?"              A: department head
    …  147 more

  Change the chunk size:      82% → 88%   keep it
  Switch to a cheaper model:  88% → 71%   don't
  Add a reranking step:       88% → 91%   keep it, if the extra wait is fine
                                          reranking = a second pass that
                                          re-orders the retrieved chunks,
                                          most useful first

A fixed set of questions with known answers, a score you can compute, run on every change.

Without it you cannot tell a real improvement from a change that made things worse.

An evaluation set — a test set — is not a complicated object: a fixed list of questions with known correct answers, a way of scoring an answer against the expected one, and the discipline to run the whole list on every change. You build it by hand, once, from questions real staff actually ask. A hundred to a few hundred is a working size.

The three rows at the bottom are the entire argument. Change the chunk size and accuracy goes from 82 to 88 per cent: keep it. Switch to a cheaper model and it falls to 71: don't, whatever it saves. Add a reranking step — a second pass that re-orders the retrieved chunks so the most useful ones come first — and it reaches 91: keep it, if users can accept the extra wait. Without those numbers every one of those decisions is a matter of opinion, and the loudest person in the meeting wins.

Scoring by hand does not survive a hundred and fifty questions run several times a week, so in practice a model does the grading — LLM-as-judge. Give a second model the question, the expected answer and the produced answer, and ask whether it is right. It is standard practice, and it comes with biases of its own that you need to know about before you trust it.

The point of all of it: without a test set you cannot tell a real improvement from a change that made things worse. You can only tell that something changed.

illustrative volumes; prices change constantly

  per request
      retrieved chunks          4,000 tokens in

  × ⟨current input price⟩ + ⟨current output price⟩  =  ⟨$ / month⟩

RAG made this bigger: the chunks you retrieved are tokens too.

4,000 retrieved tokens against a 200-token question — 95% of what you send is context you added.

You are billed by the token, on the way in and on the way out, at two different prices — and output tokens typically cost several times what input tokens do. So the bill is: how many requests, times how many tokens each request moves, times the price of a token.

Put real numbers in. Eight hundred staff, three questions each per working day, twenty-two working days: about fifty-three thousand requests a month. Each request sends four thousand tokens of retrieved chunks and two hundred tokens of question and instructions, and gets back three hundred tokens of answer. That is roughly 222 million tokens in and 16 million out every month. Multiply by whatever the two prices are on the day.

Then look at where the input goes. Four thousand tokens of retrieved context against a two-hundred-token question: about ninety-five per cent of what you send is context you chose to add. RAG did that. It is not an argument against RAG — it is the reason chunk size and retrieval count are cost decisions as well as quality decisions, and why retrieving ten chunks "to be safe" is a choice with a monthly price attached.

Prices move constantly, so do not memorise them. Memorise the shape — requests × tokens × price — and the habit of running the numbers before you commit to an architecture rather than after the first invoice.

And Then It Has to Run

Concern The trade
How long users wait retrieval and reasoning both add to it
API or self-host rented and simple, or owned and cheaper at scale
Quantization store the model's numbers less precisely: smaller and faster to run, slightly worse

Every one of these is a trade, not an answer.

Three more decisions once the thing has to run for real, and each is a trade rather than an answer.

Latency. The user waits for retrieval, then for generation, and a reasoning model makes them wait longer still. Nearly every quality improvement in this lecture costs some latency.

API or self-host. Renting a model through an API is simple and immediate, and you pay per token forever. Running your own means GPUs, people and operations, and only becomes cheaper above a certain volume — though it is also what decides whether your documents ever leave your building.

Quantization. Store the model's numbers at lower precision so it is smaller and faster to run. It also gets slightly worse. How much worse, and whether that matters for your task, is something you measure with the evaluation set you just built.

None of these has a right answer in the abstract. They have right answers for a stated situation — this many users, this budget, this latency requirement, this rule about where data may live — and the skill is defending your choice against that situation.

Three failure modes, and all three are worse in the system you have just built than they were on the first slide.

It makes things up — fluently and with complete confidence. You have already seen that one.

It obeys its input. This is the one nobody expects. A model cannot tell the difference between instructions you wrote and text that arrived with the data, because by the time it reads them they are all just tokens in the same prompt. So a document that says "ignore previous instructions" is an instruction.

Read the example carefully. Someone uploads a file to a shared drive with white text buried at the bottom of page 6. Your pipeline indexes it like any other document. A month later a question about leave retrieves it — because it genuinely is about leave — and pastes it into the prompt. And your agent has an email tool.

That is indirect prompt injection. What makes it serious is that the attacker never touches your system. They only have to get a document into a folder you index.

It leaks. Anything in the context can come back out: chunks from documents this user was not cleared to read, another user's data, your own system prompt.

The pattern underneath all three is the point. Every capability added in this lecture widened the attack surface. Retrieval gave strangers a route into your prompt; tools gave your prompt a route out into the world.

Safety Is a System Property

flowchart LR
    IN["input<br/>filtering"] --> M["the model"]
    M --> OUT["output<br/>validation"]
    OUT --> TA["tool<br/>allow-list"]
    TA --> H["human confirms<br/>anything that<br/>cannot be undone"]

The model cannot be trusted to police itself.

Guardrails live around it.

There is no instruction you can add to the model that makes it safe, because the attack arrives through the same channel as your instructions. Safety is a property of the system around the model, and it is built in layers.

Input filtering, before the model: what is allowed into the prompt, from whom, and carrying whose permissions. Output validation, after it: check the shape, check the claims against the sources that were retrieved, and refuse to pass on anything that does not check out. A tool allow-list: this agent may read a leave balance and may not send email, and the safe default is that a tool does not exist unless you deliberately added it. And a human confirming anything irreversible — money moving, mail leaving the building, records being changed.

Bring it back to the assistant. It can now file leave requests, and it reads documents that anyone in the organisation can upload. Work out what it must never be allowed to do without a person in the way.

That is a question about your responsibility rather than the model's, and it is the part of this course that is about you.
