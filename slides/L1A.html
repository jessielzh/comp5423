Next Token Prediction

flowchart LR
    P["text so far"] --> M["model"]
    M --> T["next token"]
    T -- "append, then run again" --> P

  round 1   "The capital of France is"          →   " Paris"
  round 2   "The capital of France is Paris"    →   "."
  round 3   "The capital of France is Paris."   →   ⟨stop⟩

A language model does one thing: given the text so far, it predicts the next token. That token is appended to the text, and the model runs again on the longer text. The diagram is that loop.

The three rounds below it are the loop unrolled. Round 1 takes "The capital of France is" and produces " Paris". Round 2 is fed the model's own output and produces ".". Round 3 emits a stop token, and generation ends.

That is the entire model. There is no separate "understanding" module, no knowledge database, no reasoning engine — one operation, fed back into itself. Everything else in an AI product is built around that.

Two consequences follow directly from the loop. The model cannot plan its ending before it starts, because each token is chosen with only the text before it in view. And it never "knows" the answer in advance of writing it — the answer only comes into existence as it is written out.

The Objective Is Not New

the same objective, different machinery

n-gram model (1990s–2010) Transformer LLM (2017–)
Objective predict the next word predict the next token
How counting, from a big table learned vectors + attention
Context n−1 words (an n-gram model, n = 3 by default) 100,000+ tokens
Used for scoring candidates inside speech recognition and translation generating text

The objective is old. The representation and the context are what changed.

Claude Shannon, A Mathematical Theory of Communication (1948) — the n-gram
approximations of English that started all of this.

The idea on the previous slide is the oldest one in the field. Shannon was predicting the next letter of English by hand in 1948, and by the 1990s the standard tool for it was the n-gram model.

An n-gram model predicts by counting. How often is "France is" followed by "Paris"? Count how often that phrase appears in a large body of text, and how often "Paris" is what comes next; the ratio is the prediction. Word sequences that never appeared got a fallback estimate — smoothing — which was necessary because most sequences never appear even once.

Read the table across. The objective row is the same operation in both columns — predict what comes next, append it, move along — and that is the point of the slide: what an LLM does was not invented in 2017. The one difference is the unit. An n-gram model worked in whole words; an LLM works in tokens, and what a token actually is comes next. Every row below the objective changed outright.

Three limits made the count table a dead end. It could not share anything between similar words — "cat" and "kitten" were unrelated rows, so seeing "the cat sat on the mat" taught it nothing at all about "the kitten sat on the mat". Its context was fixed and tiny: an n-gram looks only at the n−1 words before the one it is predicting, so a trigram, the usual default, sees two words and nothing else, and a 5-gram — the practical ceiling — sees four. And the table was mostly empty: each extra word of context multiplies the number of possible sequences by the size of the vocabulary, so nearly every entry has a count of zero and the prediction falls back on a guess rather than on evidence.

The last row is why nobody thought of these as writing machines. A language model was a component inside a larger system: a speech recogniser or a translation system produced a list of candidate sentences, and the language model's job was to say which candidate sounded most like real language. If you generate text from an n-gram model it stays plausible for about ten words and then loses the thread — so generation was not a use anybody pursued.

What closed the gap is the rest of this section: a representation in which similar words share structure, and an architecture that can hold a whole document in view at once.

Token

Word to tokens, o200k_base. The dot marks a token boundary.

  "the"            →  the                       1 token
  "tokenization"   →  token · ization           2 tokens
  "unbelievable"   →  un · bel · ievable        3 tokens
  "hippopotamus"   →  hip · pop · ot · amus     4 tokens
  "Jurafsky"       →  Jur · af · sky            3 tokens
  "自然语言处理"     →  自然 · 语言 · 处理          3 tokens
  "香港理工大学"     →  香港 · 理 · 工 · 大学       4 tokens

The model sees tokens instead of letters or words.

A hard question: "how many p's in hippopotamus?"

The model does not see letters, and it does not see words. It sees tokens: chunks of text drawn from a fixed list — a vocabulary of maybe 100,000 to 200,000 entries.

That list is built by BPE — Byte-Pair Encoding, and it is worth knowing how, because otherwise the splits below look random. Take a huge amount of text and break it into single characters. Find the adjacent pair that occurs most often — say t followed by h — and merge it into a single new entry, th. Count again, merge the next most frequent pair, and repeat tens of thousands of times, until the list is the size you want. Frequent sequences get promoted into entries of their own; rare ones never do.

Tokenizing new text replays those merges in the order they were learned. Everything starts as single characters, and each merge rule is applied wherever it fits. A word common enough to have been merged all the way up comes out whole; anything else stops partway and arrives in pieces.

The examples show what that produces. Common words like "the" have their own entry, so they survive whole. Rarer strings do not, so they shatter into pieces that are in the list: "tokenization" into token · ization, "hippopotamus" into four fragments, the name "Jurafsky" into three. The splits are not linguistic — "un · bel · ievable" cuts straight across the real morpheme boundary. The tokenizer is matching frequent character sequences, nothing more.

Chinese works the same way. 自然 · 语言 · 处理 happen to be three entries. 香港理工大学 splits unevenly into 香港 · 理 · 工 · 大学 — 香港 and 大学 earned their own entries in the list, 理 and 工 did not.

This is what makes the question on the slide hard. Asked how many p's are in hippopotamus, the model is looking at four opaque chunks, not twelve letters. Counting r's in strawberry and reversing a string have the same root cause. None of these are reasoning failures: the model is being asked about something it genuinely cannot see.

Current chat products often get strawberry right now. The perception limit has not gone away — something downstream compensates for it. A reasoning model may write the word out character by character in its scratchpad, where each hyphenated letter becomes its own token and is therefore visible, or the product calls a code tool to do the counting.

Try "natural language", "naturallanguage" and "language", and watch what happens to the token for language.

The same meaning costs a different number of tokens depending on the language and on which vocabulary is used.

Top block, cl100k_base (100,277 entries). The English sentence is 16 tokens, and nearly every token is a whole word with its leading space attached. That detail matters: the space belongs to the token that follows it, which is why " language" and "language" are two different entries.

The same sentence in Chinese, on the same vocabulary, is 29 tokens — for the same meaning. Two things go wrong at once. Most characters get one token each (自, 然, 语, 言), and only a few frequent pairs like 处理 earned a joint entry. Worse, the four starred characters have no entry at all, and each of them costs several tokens on its own. That is how twenty-five characters come to twenty-nine tokens. Being absent from the vocabulary is not free — it is the most expensive thing that can happen to a piece of text.

Bottom block, o200k_base (200,019 entries). The same Chinese sentence is now 16 tokens. Same sentence, same model family, a different list.

All of this is reproducible at tiktokenizer.vercel.app: paste text, switch the vocabulary in the selector, and watch the count change.

A tokenizer's vocabulary is a design choice, not a fixed property of a language.

o200k_base's extra ~100k entries over cl100k_base went mostly to non-English text and to code.

The names are just vocabulary sizes. cl100k is a 100,000-entry list, o200k a 200,000-entry one. Both are OpenAI's, both are built with BPE (Byte-Pair Encoding), the most common method for constructing a vocabulary. The second is not smarter than the first — it is bigger, and the extra ~100,000 entries were spent deliberately, mostly on non-English text and on code.

That is the whole explanation for the halved Chinese count. Nothing about Chinese changed. Somebody doubled the vocabulary and spent the new space on Chinese, so 自然 became one token instead of two. "Chinese costs more with LLMs" was true, and is no longer true. A tokenizer is a design decision, and it lands directly on your bill — which is also a lesson about this field in general: date your received wisdom, because a lot of it has expired.

The table's point is not the numbers but the pattern: every lab picks its own vocabulary. A token count is only meaningful next to the name of the tokenizer that produced it. Claude's tokenizer is not published, so Claude tokens are counted through the API rather than locally.

Why any of this matters in practice: you are charged by the token, on input and on output, at two different prices, and output is typically several times dearer. Rates change, so hold on to the consequence rather than the number — a long document pasted into a prompt is a large but cheap bill, while a model that rambles is a small but expensive one.

Token ID

cl100k_base, real output.

  "cat"      →  token #4719
  "kitten"   →  token #74 · #23257     (k · itten — two rows, not one)
  "dog"      →  token #18964

A token ID is a row number in the vocabulary. It does not carry meaning.

Question: "cat" and "kitten" carry similar meaning — how do we represent that?

A token ID is a row number in the vocabulary, and nothing more. "cat" is entry #4719, "dog" is #18964, and "kitten" is not a single entry at all — it comes back as two rows, #74 and #23257, the pieces k · itten.

Those numbers carry no meaning. #4719 and #18964 are not near or far from each other in any useful sense; the ordering is an accident of how the list was built. By the numbers alone there is nothing to say that cat is more like kitten than like dog.

That is because BPE — the merge algorithm that builds the vocabulary — is pure statistics on character frequency. It knows nothing about meaning. It merged 自 and 然 because that pair is frequent in the text it was built from, not because it understood the word.

So meaning does not come from the tokenizer. It comes from the next step.

Embedding

position carries meaning; so does direction.

Paris − France + Germany ≈ Berlin

An embedding maps each token to a vector. The mapping is learned during training.

An embedding maps each token to a vector — concretely, a few thousand numbers, with one row per vocabulary entry. Nobody wrote that table by hand: it fell out of training. Because the model is scored on predicting the next token, tokens that appear in the same contexts get pushed towards similar numbers. Meaning is a side effect of the prediction task.

The two figures show the same table from two angles.

The left one is about distance: words used in similar ways land near each other. cat sits among kitten, feline, meow and pet; dog and puppy are close but distinct; vehicles form their own cluster far away. Nearness here means "used similarly".

The right one is the stronger claim: direction carries meaning too. The step that takes you from France to Paris is the same step that takes you from Germany to Berlin, so a relationship like "capital of" shows up as a consistent displacement in the space. That is why the arithmetic under the figure works — you can do arithmetic on words.

Two honest caveats on that second figure. The result is cleanest in word2vec, the small dedicated embedding models of the 2010s; inside a modern LLM's token table it is present but messier. And it works best on words that are a single token.

flowchart LR

Tokenizer is an algorithm. Embedding is learned.

The order is the thing to hold on to: text → tokenizer → token IDs (no meaning) → embedding table → vectors (meaning).

Two of those steps are different in kind. The tokenizer is an algorithm — fixed statistics, decided before training ever starts — and what it produces is row numbers. The embedding table is learned during training, and that is where meaning enters the system.

Walk the earlier example through it once: "cat" becomes #4719 becomes a row of a few thousand numbers. Only that last step knows anything about cats.

Context Further Defines the Meaning

same word, different meanings

  "he sat on the river bank."              "he opened a bank account."

  "The animal didn't cross the street      "The animal didn't cross the street
   because it was too tired."               because it was too wide."

Same word. Same token ID. Same vector.

Question: how should the model tell the two meanings apart?

The embedding table gives every token a context-free meaning: one fixed row, identical for every occurrence — roughly everything that has been learned about that token, averaged over all the ways it gets used. That is genuinely useful and genuinely not enough.

The top pair is why. bank has two unrelated senses, but it is the same word, the same token ID, and therefore the same starting vector in both sentences. The bottom pair makes the same point about reference: one word changes — tired to wide — and what it refers to flips from the animal to the street. Nothing else in the sentence moved.

Attention is the step that specialises the vector. Same starting point, different neighbours, different result. The analogy that works: the embedding table is the dictionary entry; attention is the sense that word is carrying in this particular sentence.

One question that comes up here: if attention looks at the other tokens, what were embeddings for? Attention does not look at tokens — it looks at their vectors. Row numbers cannot be weighed against one another or blended. Embedding is what turns a row number into something attention can do arithmetic with.

And this happens repeatedly, not once. A model is a stack of 30 to 100 layers, so each token's vector is rewritten 30 to 100 times, each pass with a wider view of the sentence. That is what "depth" buys.

Attention Is All You Need

Each word looks at other words (context) with different weights.

Each token's vector is rewritten as a weighted blend of the vectors around it.

The single idea the architecture is built from: each token's vector is rewritten as a weighted blend of the vectors around it. Every token looks at the others and weighs how relevant each one is; the heavier the weight, the more that neighbour contributes to the rewritten vector.

The two figures are the same sentence with one word changed. On the left — "the animal didn't cross the street because it was too tired" — it puts its weight on animal. On the right, tired becomes wide, and it now weighs street instead.

That flip is the whole demonstration. The grammar is identical in both sentences, so no grammar rule can decide which thing it points to. What settles it is which word is relevant given everything else present — and that is exactly what the weights encode.

The weights drawn here are illustrative; how they are actually computed is a separate topic.

Transformer

the architecture from the original paper

"Attention Is All You Need" (2017) — Multi-Head Attention and N× are where capability comes from.

This is the real architecture, not a simplification — the diagram from the original paper. Encoder on the left, decoder on the right, and the N× on each side meaning the whole block is stacked many times over.

Multi-head attention is the "each vector is rewritten using the others" step from the last slide, run several times in parallel on different projections of the same vectors, then combined. Masked multi-head attention, in the decoder, means a position may only attend to itself and to earlier positions — it cannot look ahead, because at generation time the later tokens do not exist yet. The decoder's second attention block attends from the decoder's own vectors to the encoder's output; that is the arrow crossing from one half to the other.

Encoder-decoder is the shape for translation-style tasks: source language in, target language out. A decoder-only, GPT-style model drops the encoder half entirely and keeps only the masked decoder stack, reading and generating the same sequence. Most of the models in everyday use are that second kind — not every LLM has both halves.

Each block works on the output of the block below it, and the embedding table is read once, at the very bottom, never again. Early layers pick up local structure; later layers carry meaning and intent. Depth is where capability comes from — that is what N× is buying.

Add & norm and positional encoding are supporting machinery. Today the labelled attention blocks and the stacking are what to read off the diagram.

Building a Large Language Model

3 stages to build an LLM

flowchart LR
    A["Pretraining<br/><span style='font-size:.75em;color:var(--dim)'>the internet</span>"] --> B["Supervised Fine-Tuning<br/><span style='font-size:.75em;color:var(--dim)'>worked examples</span>"]
    B --> C["Alignment<br/><span style='font-size:.75em;color:var(--dim)'>preference pairs</span>"]

Three stages, in this order, each one with a different training mechanism.

A large language model is not trained once. It is built in three stages, in this order, and each stage uses different data and a different training mechanism: pretraining on the internet, supervised fine-tuning on worked examples, and alignment on preference pairs.

The assistant you use every day is the output of all three, and most people assume it is one thing trained one way. Separating the stages is what makes a model's behaviour explainable: what it knows comes from stage 1, that it answers you at all comes from stage 2, and how it answers comes from stage 3.

Each of the next three slides is one stage.

Stage 1: read an enormous fraction of the internet, predict the next token, repeat — for months, across thousands of GPUs. Data quality beats data volume.

What that produces is something that continues text, not something that answers you. A stage-1-only model asked "What is the capital of France?" is liable to reply "What is the capital of Germany? What is the capital of Italy?" — it is not refusing; it is doing exactly its job, continuing a text that looks like a list of quiz questions. Closing that gap is what stages 2 and 3 are for.

The list on the slide is, roughly, everyone on earth who does this from scratch: a few dozen organisations, because of what the compute costs. Two things to notice about it. It is short — almost nobody trains a model from scratch. And half of it is Chinese; these are realistic employers.

A handful of people build these models. Everyone else in the industry works on the other side of the API — which is not a lesser job, it is a different and much larger one.

Stage 2. Supervised Fine-Tuning

prompt and response pairs. tens of thousands of these. written by people.

  {
    "prompt":   "Customer message: my package arrived three days
                 late and the box was crushed.",

    "response": "I'm sorry — that's not the experience we want you
                 to have. I've started a replacement and a refund
                 for the damage; you'll get a confirmation email
                 within the hour."
  }

SFT uses prompt/response data to teach the model how to answer.
Sometimes what is taught is not a fact — it is a habit.

Stage 2: show the model tens of thousands of worked examples — prompt in, response out, written by people. Cheap next to stage 1, and this is the step that turns a text continuer into an assistant.

The slide shows one such pair. Notice what is not being learned: not that this customer's package was late. What is learned is the shape of a good answer — acknowledge, fix, concrete action, timeline. Not a fact, a habit.

Which means the data decides the habit. If every response in the training set had instead been three defensive paragraphs of policy, the result would be a model that cannot be brief or helpful, and no prompt would fix it afterwards.

And a human wrote that response. This stage is bought with labour rather than only compute, which is why data-labelling is a real industry with real employees.

Stage 3. Alignment

one preference pair. both answers are true, but one is preferred.

  prompt:  "I have a job interview next week and I'm nervous. Any advice?"

  A  "That's normal — it usually means you care about the outcome. Two things that help more than anything else: write down the five questions you'd least like to be asked and prepare those first, and say your answers out loud rather than in your head. What's the role?"

  B  "Interview anxiety is a common form of performance anxiety, characterised by physiological arousal and anticipatory worry prior to an evaluative situation. It is experienced by an estimated 90% of job candidates."

Question: Which one do you prefer?
It is not about right or wrong. It is about human preference. This is where the model's personality comes from.

Stage 3: show the model pairs of answers and say which one is better — not "which is correct", "which do people prefer." Tone, refusals, helpfulness, and the model's whole personality come from here.

Both answers on the slide are true. B is not wrong: every clause in it is accurate and it would survive fact-checking. It is simply useless to someone who is nervous about an interview. A does what was actually asked for — normalises the feeling, gives two specific things to do, and asks a question back.

Almost everyone prefers A, and that preference is the training signal. There is no fact here to be learned, only a preference, expressed by whoever wrote the training data. That is also why the same underlying model can feel completely different across two products — and why a model's "values" come from whoever wrote the preferences, not from the model itself.

RLHF and DPO are the two names for how this stage is done.

Summary

Model next token prediction
Token a meaningless ID
Embedding a meaningful vector
Attention using the context
Transformer the architecture
Training stages pretraining, SFT, alignment

Six lines, and they are the whole section. The model is next-token prediction, repeated. A token is a meaningless ID, a chunk taken from a fixed vocabulary. An embedding turns that ID into a vector, and that is where meaning lives. Attention rewrites each vector using the context around it. The transformer is the architecture that stacks that operation deep. And the model is built in three stages — pretraining for knowledge, SFT for instruction-following, alignment for personality.

All of that together is one component: a box that takes text in and produces text out.
